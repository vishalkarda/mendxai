# Dataset Origin and Recording Protocol

This file explains where the MODMA/Lanzhou audio data actually comes from, who was recruited and under what criteria, how the recordings were made, and why the 29-file session structure looks the way it does. It's the "how was this data produced" companion to [`02_metadata_schema_deep_dive.md`](02_metadata_schema_deep_dive.md), which covers what was recorded *about* each subject.

## Where the dataset comes from

The audio corpus is one modality of a larger multi-modal collection called MODMA (Multi-modal Open Dataset for Mental-disorder Analysis), assembled by Hanshu Cai and colleagues (corresponding author Bin Hu) at the Gansu Provincial Key Laboratory of Wearable Computing, Lanzhou University, China. The full MODMA project also includes EEG recordings (both a 128-electrode research-grade setup and a lightweight 3-electrode wearable setup) from an overlapping but not identical set of subjects — this repository only uses the audio sub-cohort of 52 subjects. The dataset paper describing all of this ("MODMA dataset: a Multi-modal Open Dataset for Mental-disorder Analysis") is stored locally at `backend/data/audio_lanzhou_2015/MODMA dataset-a Multi-modal Open Dataset for Mental-disorder Analysis.pdf` and should be cited as: Cai, H., Gao, Y., Sun, S., Li, N., Tian, F., Xiao, H., Li, J., Yang, Z., Li, X., Zhao, Q., Liu, Z., Yao, Z., Yang, M., Peng, H., Zhu, J., Zhang, X., Hu, X., & Hu, B. (2020). *MODMA dataset: a Multi-modal Open Dataset for Mental-disorder Analysis.* arXiv preprint arXiv:2002.09283. Access to the raw dataset requires signing a EULA through the MODMA project's own web portal — the signed copy of that agreement is discussed in [`06_license_and_compliance.md`](06_license_and_compliance.md).

A second paper, describing a modeling method built on top of this same 52-subject audio cohort ("A Novel Decision Tree for Depression Recognition in Speech", by Liu, Wang, Zhang, and Hu, also Lanzhou University), is stored alongside it at `backend/data/audio_lanzhou_2015/A Novel Decision Tree for Depression Recognition in Speech.pdf`. That paper is not a dataset description — it's prior modeling work on this exact data, and its methodology is summarized separately in [`05_companion_paper_methodology.md`](05_companion_paper_methodology.md).

## Who was recruited, and under what criteria

The audio cohort consists of 52 people: 23 patients with major depressive disorder (16 male, 7 female, ages 16–56) and 29 healthy controls (20 male, 9 female, ages 18–55). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, were between 18 and 55 (with the noted exception that some MDD patients' recorded ages extend down to 16), and had at least a primary-school education.

The two groups were recruited very differently, and the recruitment process matters for interpreting the data honestly. The depressed group was recruited from inpatients and outpatients at Lanzhou University Second Hospital, and every one of them went through a real diagnostic process: each was diagnosed or referred by at least one clinical psychiatrist, then given a structured MINI interview (the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview) confirming they met DSM-IV criteria for major depressive disorder. On top of that clinical interview, two additional gates were applied before someone could be included in the depressed group: a PHQ-9 score of at least 5 (this is important — it means PHQ-9 was not just *measured* on these subjects, it was literally part of the definition of who counts as "depressed" in this dataset, which has direct consequences for how PHQ-9 can and can't be used later — see the PHQ-9 discussion in [`02_metadata_schema_deep_dive.md`](02_metadata_schema_deep_dive.md)), and no psychotropic drug treatment in the two weeks before recording. People with other mental disorders, brain organ damage, serious physical illness, or severe suicidal tendencies were excluded from the depressed group entirely.

The healthy-control group was recruited by a fundamentally different, lighter-weight route: posters. There was no structured clinical interview equivalent to the MINI for this group — the only screening applied was an exclusion check for a personal or family history of mental disorders. Both groups shared two further exclusion criteria: alcohol or psychotropic drug abuse/dependence within the past year, and (for women) pregnancy, lactation, or current use of birth control pills.

The practical implication of this asymmetry is worth sitting with: the depressed group is a clinically confirmed sample with a hard PHQ-9 floor, while the healthy group is a self-selected poster-response sample with a much lighter negative-screening bar. That's a completely standard design for this kind of case-control study, but it does mean "MDD vs HC" in this dataset is really closer to "clinically-confirmed-depressed vs poster-responders-without-known-mental-illness-history" — not a perfectly matched, equally-screened comparison in both directions.

Every participant went through a formal informed-consent process, with ethics approval from the Ethics Committee for Biomedical Research at Lanzhou University Second Hospital (under the Declaration of Helsinki), and each was compensated roughly USD $16 for their time.

## Recording environment and equipment

Recordings were made in a quiet, soundproofed room with no electromagnetic interference, with ambient noise required to stay below 60dB. The microphone was a Neumann TLM102 (a well-regarded studio condenser microphone), feeding into an RME Fireface UCX audio interface, sampling at 44.1kHz with 24-bit depth, saved as uncompressed WAV. Subjects sat roughly 20cm from the microphone and were asked not to touch the equipment during recording. Our own audit of the raw files (see [`04_eda_findings_and_statistics.md`](04_eda_findings_and_statistics.md)) confirms every valid file in the corpus is indeed 44.1kHz, mono, 24-bit PCM — the documented protocol matches what's actually on disk.

The dataset paper includes its own informal quality self-assessment: it reports the recordings' signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) falls in the range of 20–30dB, describing this as "an informal and rough indication," not a rigorously validated metric. That's a useful calibration point — it tells you the source researchers were aware recording quality wasn't studio-perfect and gave a rough number rather than overclaiming precision. It's also consistent with our own independent finding that these recordings sit at a low overall level relative to full digital scale (peak levels around -40dBFS on average) — a real, measured property of the corpus that any future signal-processing work needs to account for (again, see [`04_eda_findings_and_statistics.md`](04_eda_findings_and_statistics.md) for how that affected our own silence-detection method).

After recording, the dataset creators manually segmented and labeled every session, keeping only the portions where the participant themselves was speaking (interviewer speech, silences between prompts, etc. were cut out). That manual segmentation is why each subject ends up with exactly 29 discrete files rather than one continuous 25-minute recording.

## The 25-minute session: three speaking styles, three emotional valences

The experimental design behind the 29-file structure crosses two factors: *speaking style* (interview, word reading, picture description, plus a passage-reading task) and *emotional valence* (positive, neutral, negative). The full session takes about 25 minutes and is conducted in Chinese. The order of differently-valenced prompts within each task was randomized per subject to avoid ordering effects. Here is what each task actually consists of, and why it was designed that way:

**Interview (files 01–18, 18 files).** Eighteen interview questions, drawn from DSM-IV diagnostic criteria and the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HRSD/HAM-D) question content, plus some additional scale-derived material. Six questions each for positive, neutral, and negative valence (files 1–6, 7–12, 13–18 respectively). An example prompt: "If you have a vacation, please describe your travel plans" (positive); another asks about the best gift the person has ever received and how it made them feel. This is the largest single block of data per subject and the one most directly modeled on standard clinical-interview content.

**Passage reading (file 19, 1 file).** A single fixed passage — "The North Wind and the Sun," sourced from the International Phonetic Association's own reference materials. This fable is a standard, widely-used passage in cross-linguistic phonetic and clinical acoustic research specifically because it contains a broad, representative range of phonemes in a short span, making it a good normalized point of comparison across speakers and languages. Everyone reads exactly the same text, so any differences here are less confounded by content choice than the interview or picture-description tasks.

**Word reading (files 20–25, 6 files).** Two sets of ten Chinese words each — again split across positive/neutral/negative valence — drawn from an affective-ontology word corpus and a Chinese sentiment-word extremum table. Because these are short, discrete word tokens rather than continuous speech, they're a different kind of acoustic sample than the interview or passage tasks: shorter, more controlled, less influenced by conversational prosody.

**Picture description (files 26–29, 4 files).** Subjects describe four images out loud: three images (positive, neutral, negative) drawn from the Chinese Facial Affective Picture System (CFAPS), and a fourth, specifically file 29, which is the "crying woman" image from Henry Murray's 1935 Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) — a classic projective-psychology stimulus historically used to elicit emotionally-loaded narrative responses. Picture-description tasks tend to elicit more spontaneous, less scripted speech than reading tasks, closer in character to the interview but anchored to a fixed visual stimulus rather than open conversation.

This structure is the direct source of the file-index → task-type mapping already recorded as a quick-reference table in `DATA_CONTEXT.md` section 3 — this file is where the *reasoning* behind that table lives, in case you ever need to explain to someone else (or your future self) why file 19 is treated differently from files 1–18, or why file 29 specifically is worth a second look as an emotionally distinct stimulus.
