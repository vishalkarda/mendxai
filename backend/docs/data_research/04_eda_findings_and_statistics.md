# EDA Findings and Statistics

This file is a narrative recap of what our own four rebuilt EDA notebooks (`backend/notebooks/01_dataset_audit.ipynb` through `04_feature_baseline_eda.ipynb`) actually found when run against the real data, tying the numbers together into a story rather than leaving them scattered across four separate notebooks. Every number here was produced by an executed notebook cell, not estimated — if you want to re-verify any of them, the notebook and cell are named alongside the finding.

## Structural integrity: mostly clean, with one real defect

The corpus is exactly what the protocol promises: 52 subjects, each with 29 correctly-named `.wav` files (`01.wav` through `29.wav`, no gaps, no extras, no unexpected filenames), for 1508 files total. That uniformity was verified per-subject, not assumed, in `01_dataset_audit.ipynb`.

There is one genuine data-quality defect, and it was only found because this rebuild added real corrupt-file detection (the original notebook never checked file readability at all): subject `02010004`'s files `24.wav` through `28.wav` — five files spanning the tail of the word-reading task and the start of the picture-description task — are unreadable. `soundfile` reports "Format not recognised" for all five; they have plausible non-zero file sizes (2–4.5MB) but no valid RIFF/WAVE header. No exact-duplicate files exist anywhere in the corpus (checked via a content fingerprint across all 1508 files). Every downstream notebook excludes these 5 files explicitly rather than silently failing on them.

## Metadata joins cleanly, and the class/demographic balance is as documented

Joining the 52 on-disk subject folders against the 52 rows in the metadata workbook (using the zero-padding recipe described in `02_metadata_schema_deep_dive.md`) produces a perfect 1:1 match — zero orphans on either side, hard-asserted in `01_dataset_audit.ipynb` so this would fail loudly if the data ever changed. Class balance: 23 MDD subjects, 29 HC subjects. Gender: 36 male, 16 female overall, split as 20 HC/16 MDD male and 9 HC/7 MDD female. Age ranges 18–52 with a mean around 31.3, and — importantly — does *not* differ meaningfully between MDD and HC groups (Cohen's d ≈ -0.07, p ≈ 0.75 in `02_clinical_metadata_eda.ipynb`), meaning age isn't a confound for any acoustic comparison drawn from this dataset.

## Which metadata scales actually separate the two classes

`02_clinical_metadata_eda.ipynb` ran a Mann-Whitney U test plus Cohen's d for every numeric metadata column, comparing MDD against HC. Ranked by effect size: PHQ-9 separates most strongly (d ≈ 4.66) but is excluded as a candidate feature because a PHQ-9 score of 5 or higher was literally required for MDD inclusion — see the full explanation in `02_metadata_schema_deep_dive.md`. After PHQ-9, GAD-7 (anxiety severity, d ≈ 3.74) and PSQI (sleep quality, d ≈ 2.37) are the strongest legitimate separators, followed by SSRS (social support, d ≈ -1.42), education years (d ≈ -1.23), CTQ-SF (childhood trauma history, d ≈ 1.09), and LES (life events, d ≈ -1.02). All of these are clinically unsurprising directions — anxiety, poor sleep, low social support, trauma history, and stressful life events are all well-established correlates of depression — but they're worth having as concrete, dataset-specific numbers rather than assumed.

## Audio signal quality: a real methodological lesson, not just a QA checklist

`03_audio_signal_eda.ipynb` decoded all 1503 valid files (not a single sample file per subject, which is what the original, pre-rebuild notebook did). Sample rate, channel count, and bit depth are perfectly uniform: 44100Hz, mono, 24-bit PCM across every valid file, matching the documented recording protocol exactly. Duration varies by task type as expected — interview files have a median around 12.6 seconds, the single passage-reading file per subject runs about 42 seconds, word-reading files average about 12.9 seconds, and picture-description files average about 16.4 seconds — with 12 files flagged as statistical outliers within their own task-type group (mostly unusually long interview responses).

The more interesting finding is about recording level, and it's a good example of why decoding the full corpus mattered rather than sampling one file. These recordings sit at a genuinely low level relative to digital full scale — average peak level is around -40dBFS, nowhere near 0dBFS. The first version of the silence-detection logic in this rebuild used a fixed absolute threshold (flag any audio frame quieter than -40dBFS as "silence"), and because the *entire corpus* sits near or below that level, it flagged essentially 100% of every file as silent — technically correct given the threshold, but useless as a QA signal. The fix was to measure silence *relative to each file's own peak* (a frame counts as silent if it's more than 20dB quieter than that file's loudest frame), which produces sane, interpretable results: about 40% of frames across the corpus (601 of 1503 files, by a >50%-of-frames-silent criterion) show substantial pause content, consistent with ordinary interview-style speech with natural pauses, not a defect. No clipping was found in any file, in either version of the check. On interview files specifically, HC subjects show a slightly higher average silence ratio than MDD subjects (≈0.41 vs ≈0.36) — a small, exploratory difference in the *opposite* direction from a naive "depressed speakers pause more" hypothesis, worth treating as a question for future work rather than a conclusion given the small sample.

## Acoustic features: real signal, but weaker than the metadata scales

`04_feature_baseline_eda.ipynb` extracted 70 handcrafted acoustic/prosodic features (MFCCs, spectral shape, pause/energy statistics) from the 1491 files that passed the duration-outlier filter, then aggregated to the subject level — 52 rows, mean and standard deviation of every feature per subject — because a single subject's ~29 recordings are correlated with each other, not independent samples, and analyzing at the file level would make separation look artificially stronger than it really is. At the subject level, the strongest acoustic separators are all MFCC-family features: `mfcc12_min_mean` (d ≈ 1.22), `mfcc12_mean_mean` (d ≈ 1.18), and `mfcc5_std_mean` (d ≈ -1.13) lead the ranking, with spectral bandwidth also showing up further down (d ≈ 0.90).

Put side by side with the metadata findings above, the pattern is clear and expected: self-report clinical scales (GAD-7, PSQI) separate the two groups more strongly than handcrafted acoustic features do. That's not a failure of the acoustic features — voice-based depression signals are known to be real but noisy and multi-dimensional — it's just a reminder that acoustic modeling on this dataset should be judged against a modest, realistic bar, not against how cleanly the self-report scales separate. The one external benchmark available for that judgment comes from the companion methods paper covered in `05_companion_paper_methodology.md`: a purpose-built decision-tree fusion method, using a much larger 1609-dimensional feature set and per-segment classifiers, reached 75.8% accuracy on male subjects and 68.5% on female subjects — a reasonable target for any future baseline model built from this repository's feature-extraction pipeline.
