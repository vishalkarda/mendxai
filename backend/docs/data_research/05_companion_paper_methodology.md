# Companion Paper Methodology: Prior Art for Modeling

The paper "A Novel Decision Tree for Depression Recognition in Speech" (Liu, Wang, Zhang, and Hu, Lanzhou University; stored at `backend/data/audio_lanzhou_2015/A Novel Decision Tree for Depression Recognition in Speech.pdf`) is not the dataset description — it's a full modeling study built on the exact same 52-subject audio cohort this repository uses. Its headline numbers (75.8% accuracy on male subjects, 68.5% on female subjects) are already cited elsewhere in this project's documentation as an external benchmark, but the *how* behind those numbers is genuinely useful prior art for anyone building a baseline model in this repository, so it's worth understanding in full rather than just the accuracy figures.

## Features: a much larger, more engineered feature set than this repo's current baseline

The paper's classifiers are trained on a 1609-dimensional feature vector per speech segment (i.e., per one of the 29 recordings), built from several distinct families of features stacked together:

- The bulk of the dimensionality comes from features extracted with **openSMILE**, a standard speech-feature-extraction toolkit: MFCCs, linear predictive coefficients (LPC), jitter (a measure of pitch-period irregularity), and correlation statistics derived from all of these.
- A small, manually engineered **"tremor" feature set** (8 dimensions), computed from correlation statistics of a 15Hz amplitude/fundamental-frequency modulation — aimed at capturing fine-grained vocal micro-tremor that isn't naturally exposed by standard MFCC/LPC extraction.
- A **pause and silence feature set**, derived from speech endpoint detection on each segment: maximum pause duration, reaction time, total recording time, total vocalization time, total pause time, number of pauses, mean pause length, percent pause time, and a speech-to-pause ratio. The paper's stated rationale is direct: "each pause reflects the brain response of the speaker at that time," and pause behavior is treated as a plausible marker of the psychomotor slowing associated with depression.
- **Three energy-related features** (overall energy, low-frequency energy ratio, and a temporal-time feature), included specifically because amplitude/energy change over time is treated as a candidate depression signal.

This repository's current `feature_extractor.py` produces a much smaller, more standard 70-feature set per file (MFCC statistics, spectral shape, pause/energy statistics — see `04_eda_findings_and_statistics.md` for what actually got extracted and how it performed). The companion paper's much richer feature set, especially the explicit tremor and detailed pause-timing features, is a natural place to look for feature-engineering ideas if a future baseline needs to close the gap with the paper's reported accuracy.

## Preprocessing and feature selection

Before feature extraction, the paper applies a standard pre-emphasis filter (`H(z) = 1 - μz⁻¹`, with μ = 0.97) to boost high-frequency signal content, then frames the signal into 25-millisecond windows using a Hamming window. After extracting the full 1609-dimensional feature vector, the paper applies the **Relief algorithm** for feature selection: it repeatedly samples a feature, finds its nearest neighbor within the same class and its nearest neighbor in the opposite class, and updates a running weight for that feature based on how well it discriminates between those neighbors, discarding low-weight features below a threshold. The paper's stated reasons for choosing Relief specifically are that it preserves the original physical meaning of each feature (unlike, say, a PCA-based dimensionality reduction, which would produce uninterpretable linear combinations), and that it's simpler and more computationally efficient than alternative selection methods. This selection step reduces the 1609-dimensional feature vector down to the **top 20 features** per cross-validation fold.

## Classification and the paper's actual novel contribution

Two base classifiers were compared on the 20 selected features: SVM and Random Forest, evaluated with 4-fold cross-validation (3 folds train, 1 fold test), averaged over 50 repeated randomized runs. SVM performed comparably to, and slightly better than, Random Forest, so SVM became the base per-segment classifier feeding into the paper's real contribution: a way of combining 29 separate per-segment predictions (one per recording) into a single subject-level verdict.

The paper actually describes three fusion approaches, and comparing them is informative:

1. **Simple majority voting** — tally the 29 per-segment SVM predictions for a subject and take the majority class. This is the naive baseline.
2. **A prior binary-tree method** (from earlier literature, used here as a second baseline) — builds a decision tree over segments ordered by *raw accuracy*: the root is the single most accurate segment-level model; if a subject is predicted "normal" at a node, the next node consulted is the highest-*specificity* remaining segment; if predicted "depressed," the next node is the highest-*sensitivity* remaining segment.
3. **The paper's own novel method** — the same tree-of-segments idea, but ordered by *sensitivity and specificity* from the start rather than raw accuracy: the root is the *most sensitive* segment-model (best at correctly catching true depression cases), and subsequent nodes are chosen the same way as the prior method (most-specific after a "normal" verdict, most-sensitive after a "depressed" verdict). The tree stops and commits to a final verdict once either the "healthy" or "depression" count reaches 2, capped at a maximum depth of 4 layers — a shallow, fast structure, deliberately kept simple given how small the subject pool is. The reasoning given for choosing sensitivity/specificity over raw accuracy as the tree-construction criterion is that these two measures play complementary roles closer to how a clinician actually reasons through successive diagnostic evidence — sensitivity as "how well does this catch real cases," specificity as "how well does this rule healthy people out" — rather than optimizing a single blended accuracy number.

This third method is what produces the reported 75.8%/68.5% figures.

## Per-task performance, and what it implies about which recordings carry the most signal

The paper reports accuracy broken down by task type (all/male/female): interview 56.8/59.2/54.2%, passage reading 62.0/63.6/57.6%, word reading 54.2/58.4/61.0%, picture description 54.2/58.0/60.1%. The passage-reading task — despite being just one fixed, short recording per subject, versus the interview's 18 files — consistently outperforms every other task type. The paper's own interpretation is that the fixed reading passage removes content variability between subjects (everyone reads the identical text), which lets pause and timing features do their work more cleanly, and that depressed subjects show measurably slower response/reading behavior when confronted with unfamiliar text — exactly the kind of signal the pause-feature set (described above) is designed to capture. That's a useful piece of intuition if you're deciding where to invest feature-engineering effort in this repository: the passage-reading file (index 19 in this dataset's numbering) may be disproportionately informative relative to its size.

## Caveats the paper gives about its own results

Three points from the paper are worth carrying forward honestly rather than treating the 75.8%/68.5% numbers as a fixed target: individual interview segments varied a lot in how predictive they were — the paper notes the 6th and 11th interview questions specifically outperformed other interview segments, suggesting segment-level feature investigation matters more than treating all interview files uniformly; female-subject models generalized noticeably worse than male-subject models, which the paper attributes to the smaller female sample (7 depressed vs. 16 male depressed in this cohort — see `01_origin_and_protocol.md` for the full recruitment numbers), a class-imbalance caveat that applies directly to any future gender-stratified modeling in this repository; and the paper explicitly states that "the number of subjects is small, which may reduce the generalization ability of the model" — i.e., the authors themselves flag that their own headline numbers may not be fully stable or reproducible at this sample size. That's a reasonable, honest note to carry into any future modeling work on this same 52-subject cohort.
