# Metadata Schema Deep Dive

`DATA_CONTEXT.md` lists the 11 real columns in `subjects_information_audio_lanzhou_2015.xlsx` as a flat table. This file explains what each of them actually *is*: what it measures, how it's scored, and — where relevant — why you should or shouldn't trust it as a model feature. Read this alongside [`01_origin_and_protocol.md`](01_origin_and_protocol.md), which covers how and why these subjects were recruited in the first place.

## Identity and demographic columns

**`subject id`** is stored in the workbook as a plain integer with no leading zero (e.g. `2010002`), while the corresponding audio folder on disk is a zero-padded 8-digit string (`02010002`). This isn't a data error — it's just Excel's default integer formatting stripping the leading zero — but it means every join between the workbook and the audio files has to re-pad the ID with `str(id).zfill(8)` before comparing. That recipe is verified (52/52 match, zero orphans on either side) and lives in code in `_eda_utils.py` and `data_loader.py`, and as a documented fact in `DATA_CONTEXT.md` section 5.

**`type`** is the class label: `MDD` for the depressed group, `HC` for the healthy-control group. This is the single most important column in the whole workbook, and it has its own dedicated write-up — see [`03_label_terminology_history.md`](03_label_terminology_history.md) — because the rest of this codebase has, at various points, called the control group `NC` instead, and that confusion is worth understanding properly rather than just memorizing "it's HC now."

**`age`** and **`gender`** are self-explanatory demographic fields, but worth calling out: our own EDA (see [`04_eda_findings_and_statistics.md`](04_eda_findings_and_statistics.md)) found age does *not* differ meaningfully between the MDD and HC groups, which is reassuring — a large age gap between groups would confound any acoustic comparison, since voice characteristics change with age independent of mental health status. Gender, on the other hand, *is* imbalanced (36 male, 16 female overall), which matters both for statistical power within each gender and because the companion modeling paper reports separate, meaningfully different accuracy for male versus female subjects (see [`05_companion_paper_methodology.md`](05_companion_paper_methodology.md)).

**`education（years）`** records years of formal education. Note the column name itself contains a full-width parenthesis character (`（` `）` rather than the ASCII `(` `)`) — a small but real detail, almost certainly a byproduct of the workbook being originally authored in a Chinese-locale version of Excel. If you ever reference this column by name in code, you need the exact full-width characters or the lookup will silently fail.

## The six clinical scales

Only one of these six scales has its purpose explained anywhere in the source papers. The other five are present in the workbook with no stated rationale — they were evidently collected as standard supplementary clinical/demographic covariates, which is common practice in psychiatric research, but it means their inclusion here is a matter of clinical convention rather than something the MODMA team argued for in writing. That distinction matters: it's honest to explain what each scale measures using general clinical knowledge of the instrument, while being clear that *why this specific study collected it* is not documented.

**PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire-9)** is the one scale whose role is fully documented, because it's not just a measurement here — it's part of the literal definition of the MDD group. The PHQ-9 is a widely used, validated 9-item self-report questionnaire (each item scored 0–3, for a total range of 0–27) that maps directly onto the DSM-IV symptom criteria for major depressive disorder. Standard severity bands are roughly: 0–4 minimal, 5–9 mild, 10–14 moderate, 15–19 moderately severe, 20–27 severe. In this dataset, a PHQ-9 score of at least 5 was a *requirement* for inclusion in the MDD group (see [`01_origin_and_protocol.md`](01_origin_and_protocol.md)). Our own analysis in notebook 02 confirms this shows up starkly in the data: MDD subjects average a PHQ-9 of about 18, HC subjects about 2.5, an enormous effect size (Cohen's d ≈ 4.66) — but that separation is definitional, not discovered. Using PHQ-9 as an input feature to a depression classifier on this dataset would mean training a model to partially recover its own label-assignment rule, not to find an independent behavioral or acoustic signal. This is why the EDA notebooks explicitly flag PHQ-9 as excluded from candidate-feature consideration.

**GAD-7 (Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale)** is a companion self-report instrument to the PHQ-9, from the same research tradition, measuring anxiety symptom severity rather than depression specifically (7 items, 0–3 each, range 0–21; rough bands 0–4 minimal, 5–9 mild, 10–14 moderate, 15–21 severe). It wasn't part of the MDD inclusion criteria, which is exactly what makes it interesting: our EDA found GAD-7 is the *strongest non-leaky* separator in the whole metadata table (Cohen's d ≈ 3.74, MDD mean ≈14.3 vs HC mean ≈1.7). That's expected clinically — anxiety and depression are highly comorbid — but because it wasn't a gatekeeping criterion, it's a legitimate (if still self-report, not acoustic) signal rather than a circular one.

**PSQI (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index)** is a 19-item self-report instrument that produces a global sleep-quality score (typically summarizing seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction), with higher scores indicating worse sleep; a global score above 5 is a commonly used cutoff for "poor sleeper." It's the second-strongest non-leaky separator found in this dataset (Cohen's d ≈ 2.37) — again clinically unsurprising, since sleep disturbance is one of the core diagnostic symptoms of depression, but useful as an independent (non-PHQ-9) corroborating signal.

**CTQ-SF (Childhood Trauma Questionnaire — Short Form)** is a 28-item retrospective self-report measuring history of childhood abuse and neglect, typically broken into five subscales (emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect). It measures a risk-factor history rather than current symptoms — the idea being that childhood trauma is a well-established predictor of adult depression risk, not a symptom of a current episode. In this dataset it showed a moderate separation (Cohen's d ≈ 1.09), consistent with, but much weaker than, current-state measures like GAD-7 or PSQI.

**LES (Life Event Scale)** and **SSRS (Social Support Rating Scale)** are both standard instruments in Chinese clinical psychology research, and the values present in this workbook are consistent with their typical Chinese-language versions, though neither source paper names an exact citation for the version used here — worth flagging as inferred from convention rather than confirmed. LES-type instruments generally ask respondents to report recent stressful life events (across domains like family, work, health, and finances) and weight each by frequency and self-rated impact; because impact can be rated as either a positive or negative experience, LES scores are commonly signed rather than purely additive — which matches what's actually in this workbook (some subjects have negative LES totals, e.g. -143 or -100, meaning their weighted negative events outweighed positive ones). SSRS-type instruments measure perceived social support, typically summing across objective support (concrete help received), subjective support (felt support), and utilization of support (how much someone draws on their support network) into a total score. In this dataset, SSRS showed a moderate inverse separation (Cohen's d ≈ -1.42 — HC subjects reporting more social support than MDD subjects, the expected direction), and LES showed a smaller separation (d ≈ -1.02).

## The two columns that aren't data

The raw workbook has 13 columns total, but only 11 carry real per-subject data. `Unnamed: 11` is essentially blank. `Unnamed: 12` is not blank, but it isn't a data column either — it holds an inline legend block (near the top of the sheet) spelling out what each abbreviation stands for (MDD = Major Depressive Disorder, HC = Healthy Control, PHQ-9 = Patient Health Questionnaire, and so on down the list), plus a short remark that "the subject id of all data is unified" — i.e. the workbook's own confirmation that subject IDs here are meant to match the audio-folder naming, which is exactly the join relationship documented above. Both columns are dropped during loading (see `DataLoader.load_metadata()` in `backend/src/mendxai/ml/data_loader.py`); they're mentioned here only so a future reader doesn't wonder whether something was silently lost.
